### **EXL-89: Abdominal Crunch / Sit-up (Floor or Bench)**

## Quick Takeaway
A fundamental spinal flexion exercise targeting the rectus abdominis, but core stability exercises may be more beneficial for functional strength and spine health.

## Key Points
- Primarily targets rectus abdominis through spinal flexion movement
- Crunches limit range of motion to upper spine, while sit-ups involve full torso lifting
- Hip flexors become significantly involved during full sit-ups
- Modern research favors core stability training over repeated spinal flexion
- Proper form emphasizes controlled movement without neck strain

## Deep Dive

### Movement Mechanics
The abdominal crunch and sit-up are spinal flexion exercises that primarily target the rectus abdominis. The key difference lies in range of motion: crunches involve lifting only the head, shoulders, and upper back off the floor, while sit-ups continue the movement until the torso is nearly upright.

During crunches, the movement focuses on bringing the rib cage toward the pelvis through controlled spinal flexion. The lower back should remain in contact with the floor, minimizing hip flexor involvement. Sit-ups, however, require significant hip flexor activation to complete the full range of motion, which can shift emphasis away from the abdominals.

### Target Muscle Groups
**Primary:** Rectus abdominis drives the spinal flexion movement
**Secondary:** Internal and external obliques provide rotational stability, hip flexors (particularly active in sit-ups)
**Stabilizers:** Transverse abdominis maintains core stability throughout the movement

### Execution Technique
**Setup:** Lie supine with knees bent, feet flat and hip-width apart. Place hands lightly behind head without interlacing fingers, or cross arms over chest.

**Crunch Execution:** Exhale while contracting abdominals to lift shoulders off the floor. Focus on spinal flexion rather than lifting height. Pause briefly at peak contraction, then slowly lower under control while inhaling.

**Sit-up Execution:** Continue lifting until torso approaches vertical. This requires greater hip flexor involvement and may need foot anchoring for stability.

### Current Research Perspective
Modern exercise science increasingly questions the value of repeated spinal flexion exercises. Research suggests that excessive spinal flexion under load may contribute to disc degeneration over time. Many fitness professionals now recommend core stability exercises that challenge the abdominals while maintaining neutral spine position.

## Research Evidence

**McGill, S. (2016).** Low back disorders: evidence-based prevention and rehabilitation. *Human Kinetics*. 
- Key finding: Repeated spinal flexion exercises may contribute to disc herniation risk in susceptible individuals
- Study type: Biomechanical analysis and clinical review
- Evidence quality: High (Leading spine biomechanics research)

**Sahrmann, S. et al. (2017).** Diagnosis and treatment of movement impairment syndromes. *Mosby*.
- Key finding: Core stability exercises show superior transfer to functional activities compared to traditional crunches
- Study type: Clinical review and movement analysis
- Evidence quality: High (Established movement science principles)

## Practical Applications

### Programming Guidelines
- **Beginners:** 2-3 sets of 10-15 repetitions with focus on form
- **Progression:** Increase reps before adding external load
- **Advanced:** Consider transitioning to stability-based exercises

### Form Priorities
1. Keep movement slow and controlled (2-second up, 2-second down)
2. Exhale during lifting phase, inhale during lowering
3. Maintain constant tension on abdominals
4. Avoid momentum or bouncing movements

### Exercise Alternatives
For better functional outcomes, consider progressing to:
- Core stability exercises [[L4. Core Stability Training]]
- Planks and variations [[EXL-84 Plank (Standard & Variations)]]
- Dead bug patterns
- Anti-extension and anti-rotation exercises

## Common Mistakes
⚠️ Pulling on the neck with hands, creating cervical strain
⚠️ Using momentum or bouncing to complete repetitions
⚠️ Lifting lower back off floor during crunches (converts to sit-up)
⚠️ Relying on hip flexors instead of abdominal contraction
⚠️ Holding breath during the movement

## Progression and Regression Options

### Progressions
Following [[B1. Progressive Overload]] principles:
- Increase repetitions and sets [[B5. Training Volume and Frequency]]
- Add external load (weight plate on chest)
- Perform on decline bench for increased difficulty
- Progress to cable crunches [[EXL-101 Cable Crunch]]
- Advance to hanging leg raises [[EXL-88 Hanging Leg Raise  Knee Raise (Captain's Chair or Pull-up Bar)]]

### Regressions
- Reduce range of motion (partial crunches)
- Focus on isometric holds (hollow body position)
- Begin with planks [[EXL-84 Plank (Standard & Variations)]]
- Practice dead bug exercises for motor control

## Safety Considerations
**Contraindications:** Acute neck pain, acute low back pain (especially disc-related), diastasis recti during pregnancy/postpartum period

**Cautions:** Individuals with history of disc problems should prioritize core stability exercises [[L4. Core Stability Training]] over repeated spinal flexion movements

Proper assessment [[IX-6 Exercise Contraindications.md]] should precede exercise prescription, particularly for individuals with spinal concerns.
