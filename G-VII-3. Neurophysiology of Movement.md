### G-VII-3. Neurophysiology of Movement

## Quick Takeaway
Your nervous system controls movement through a complex network that you can train - early strength gains come from neural improvements before muscle growth, and practicing balance/coordination literally rewires your brain for better performance.

## Key Points
- Motor units follow the Size Principle: small, fatigue-resistant units activate first, then larger, more powerful units as force demands increase
- Initial strength gains (first 2-4 weeks) come from neural adaptations - better muscle recruitment and coordination, not muscle growth
- Proprioceptors act like your body's GPS system, constantly feeding back position and tension information to refine movement
- Motor learning progresses from thinking about every detail to automatic execution through deliberate practice
- Balance and coordination training physically changes neural pathways, improving reaction time and injury prevention

## Deep Dive

Movement control involves complex interactions between the central nervous system (CNS - brain, spinal cord) and peripheral nervous system (PNS - nerves connecting CNS to muscles/sensory receptors).

### Central Command Centers

**Motor Cortex** serves as mission control for voluntary movement, planning and initiating movement commands. The **cerebellum and basal ganglia** act as quality control centers, coordinating movement, maintaining balance and posture, facilitating motor learning, and smoothing out jerky movements into fluid motion.

The **spinal cord** functions as the major highway, transmitting motor commands from brain to muscles via motor neurons while housing reflex circuits that can respond instantly without brain involvement.

### Motor Units: The Building Blocks of Movement

A **motor unit** consists of a single alpha motor neuron and all the muscle fibers it innervates, representing the fundamental unit of motor control ([[A7. Muscle Physiology Fundamentals]]). Motor unit recruitment (activating more units) and rate coding (increasing firing frequency) control muscle force production following Henneman's Size Principle - smaller Type I units recruit first for fine control and endurance, then larger Type II units activate for maximum force production.

### Proprioceptive Feedback System

Proprioceptors function as your body's internal sensing network, providing continuous information about body position, movement, and force generation:

**Muscle Spindles** are located within muscle fibers and detect changes in muscle length and rate of stretch. They mediate the stretch reflex (myotatic reflex) and contribute to baseline muscle tone. When a muscle is stretched rapidly, spindles trigger a reflexive contraction to resist the stretch.

**Golgi Tendon Organs (GTOs)** are positioned in tendons and monitor muscle tension changes. They mediate the inverse stretch reflex (autogenic inhibition), protecting against excessive force by causing muscle relaxation when tension becomes dangerously high. GTOs play a crucial role in PNF stretching techniques ([[L2. Mobility and Flexibility Development]]).

**Joint Receptors** embedded in joint capsules and ligaments detect joint position, movement direction, and pressure changes, while the **vestibular system** in the inner ear detects head position and acceleration for balance control.

### Motor Learning and Skill Development

Motor learning involves neuroplasticity - actual changes in neural pathways and synapses through practice. The process follows three distinct stages:

1. **Cognitive Stage**: Understanding the task, high mental effort, inconsistent performance
2. **Associative Stage**: Refining movement patterns, fewer errors, developing feel for the skill  
3. **Autonomous Stage**: Skill becomes automatic, minimal conscious control required

Feedback proves critical throughout this process, including intrinsic feedback (what you feel) and extrinsic feedback (coaching cues, video analysis).

## Research Evidence

**Hortobágyi, T., et al. (2006).** Changes in muscle strength, muscle fibre size and myofibrillar gene expression after immobilization and retraining in humans. *Journal of Physiology*, 577(1), 239-248. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2006.107987
- Key finding: Neural adaptations account for majority of early strength gains, with muscle fiber changes occurring later
- Study type: Controlled trial
- Evidence quality: High (well-controlled human study with multiple measures)

**Gruber, M., et al. (2007).** Differential effects of ballistic versus sensorimotor training on rate of force development and neural activation in humans. *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research*, 21(1), 274-282. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1519/R-20526.1
- Key finding: Sensorimotor training improves neural drive and coordination independent of strength gains
- Study type: Randomized controlled trial
- Evidence quality: High (randomized design with neural measurements)

**Taube, W., et al. (2008).** Spinal and supraspinal adaptations associated with balance training and their functional relevance. *Acta Physiologica*, 193(2), 101-116. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1748-1716.2008.01850.x
- Key finding: Balance training induces measurable changes in spinal reflex pathways and cortical excitability
- Study type: Systematic review
- Evidence quality: High (comprehensive review of neurophysiological mechanisms)

## Practical Applications

**For Strength Training** related to [[B4. Strength Training Principles]]:
- Expect rapid strength gains in weeks 1-4 from neural improvements before visible muscle growth
- Focus on perfect technique during this neural adaptation phase to groove optimal movement patterns
- Use progressive overload to continually challenge motor unit recruitment
- Include unilateral (single-limb) exercises to enhance neural coordination between sides

**For Balance and Coordination** ([[L3. Balance and Coordination Training]]):
- Practice unstable surface training 2-3x per week to enhance proprioceptive feedback
- Include single-leg stands (start with 30 seconds, progress to 2 minutes)
- Progress from stable to unstable surfaces, slow to fast movements
- Add cognitive tasks (counting backwards) while balancing to challenge neural processing

**For Skill Development:**
- Break complex movements into smaller components during cognitive stage
- Provide frequent feedback early (every 2-3 repetitions), then reduce as skill develops
- Practice consistently - motor learning requires repetition over time, not just intensity
- Use variable practice conditions to enhance transfer to real-world situations

## Common Mistakes

⚠️ **Expecting immediate muscle growth** - Early strength gains are neural, not structural changes
⚠️ **Ignoring proprioceptive training** - Balance work isn't just for rehabilitation; it enhances all movement quality
⚠️ **Rushing through motor learning stages** - Each stage requires adequate practice time and appropriate feedback
⚠️ **Overlooking technique for load** - Neural adaptations work best when movement patterns are correct from the start
⚠️ **Training only dominant movement patterns** - Include multi-planar and unilateral exercises for complete neural development
