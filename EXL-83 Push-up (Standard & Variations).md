### **EXL-83: Push-up (Standard & Variations)**

**ID:** EXL-83-BW-PUSHUP **Created:** 2025-05-01 (Updated: 2026-05-08)

## Quick Takeaway
Push-ups are a fundamental bodyweight exercise that effectively targets the chest, shoulders, and triceps while building core stability and can be modified for all fitness levels.

## Key Points
- Targets pectoralis major, anterior deltoids, and triceps as primary movers with significant core stabilization demands
- Body position forms a straight line from head to heels with hands placed slightly wider than shoulder-width
- Elbow angle should be 45-60 degrees relative to torso to optimize joint mechanics and reduce injury risk
- Numerous variations allow progression from beginner (incline/knee push-ups) to advanced (one-arm, weighted, plyometric)
- Core engagement is critical to maintain proper spinal alignment and prevent compensatory movement patterns

## Deep Dive

### Movement Mechanics and Muscle Activation
The push-up is a closed kinetic chain exercise that requires coordinated activation of multiple muscle groups. The primary movers include the pectoralis major (chest), anterior deltoid (front shoulder), and triceps brachii (back of upper arm). Secondary movers include the serratus anterior and coracobrachialis, while extensive stabilization is required from the core musculature, glutes, and even the legs to maintain proper body alignment.

### Proper Form and Technique
**Setup:** Position hands on the floor slightly wider than shoulder-width apart. Extend legs back, supporting body weight on hands and toes. The body should form a straight line from head to heels. Engage core and glutes to prevent hip sagging or piking.

**Execution:** Inhale while lowering the chest toward the floor by bending the elbows. Keep elbows at a 45-60 degree angle relative to the torso rather than flaring them excessively wide. Lower until the chest nearly touches the floor or upper arms are parallel to the ground. Maintain the straight body line throughout the movement. Exhale while pressing forcefully through the hands to return to the starting position.

### Progression and Regression Options
**Progressions for increased difficulty:**
- Increase repetitions and sets [[B5. Training Volume and Frequency]]
- Tempo variations including slow eccentric phases [[B20. Eccentric Training]]
- Decline push-ups with feet elevated
- Close-grip or diamond push-ups for greater triceps emphasis
- Plyometric push-ups for power development
- Weighted push-ups using plates or weighted vests
- One-arm push-up progressions
- Ring push-ups for instability training [[X9. Gymnastics  Calisthenics]]

**Regressions for beginners:**
- Incline push-ups with hands elevated on a bench or wall
- Knee push-ups (modified position)
- Focus on plank variations to build core strength [[EXL-84 Plank (Standard & Variations)]]
- Transition to machine chest press [[EXL-51 Chest Press (Machine - Seated)]] or dumbbell bench press [[EXL-21 Dumbbell Bench Press (Flat)]]

## Research Evidence

**Kikuchi & Nakazato (2017).** Low-load bench press and push-up induce similar muscle hypertrophy and strength gain. *Journal of Exercise Science & Fitness*, 15(1), 37-42. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesf.2017.06.003
- Key finding: Push-ups produced similar muscle hypertrophy and strength gains compared to bench press when performed to failure
- Study type: RCT
- Evidence quality: Medium (small sample size but well-controlled design)

**Calatayud et al. (2014).** Muscle activation differences between stable push-ups and push-ups with a unilateral v-shaped suspension system at different heights. *Motriz: Revista de Educação Física*, 20(1), 87-93. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1590/S1980-65742014000100013
- Key finding: Push-up variations with instability significantly increased core muscle activation compared to standard push-ups
- Study type: Observational (EMG analysis)
- Evidence quality: Medium (controlled laboratory conditions with objective measurements)

**Ebben et al. (2011).** Muscle activation during lower body resistance training. *International Journal of Sports Medicine*, 32(1), 1-7. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0030-1268008
- Key finding: Closed kinetic chain exercises like push-ups require greater stabilizer muscle activation compared to open chain alternatives
- Study type: Observational (EMG analysis)
- Evidence quality: Medium (established methodology but limited to acute responses)

## Practical Applications

**For Beginners:**
- Start with incline push-ups using a bench or wall (higher incline = easier)
- Progress to knee push-ups when incline becomes manageable
- Build up to 3 sets of 10-15 knee push-ups before attempting standard push-ups

**For Intermediate:**
- Aim for 3 sets of 8-15 standard push-ups with perfect form
- Incorporate tempo variations (3-second descent, 1-second pause, explosive ascent)
- Add 1-2 push-up sessions per week as part of upper body training

**For Advanced:**
- Progress to decline push-ups or add external weight
- Incorporate plyometric variations for power development
- Work toward single-arm push-up progressions

**Programming Guidelines:**
- Allow 48-72 hours recovery between push-up-focused sessions
- Focus on quality over quantity - maintain perfect form throughout all repetitions
- Use push-ups as part of circuit training or as a bodyweight strength exercise

## Common Mistakes

⚠️ **Hip sagging or piking** - Indicates weak core strength; regress to plank holds or incline push-ups
⚠️ **Flaring elbows too wide** - Increases shoulder impingement risk; keep elbows at 45-60 degrees from torso
⚠️ **Incomplete range of motion** - Reduces effectiveness; lower chest to within 1-2 inches of floor
⚠️ **Forward head posture** - Creates neck strain; maintain neutral spine alignment
⚠️ **Rushing through repetitions** - Reduces muscle activation; control both eccentric and concentric phases
