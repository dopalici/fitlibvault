### **EXL-64: Hip Adduction (Machine)**

**ID:** EXL-64-MEX-HIPADD **Created:** 2025-04-30 (Updated: 2025-01-27)

## Quick Takeaway
Hip adduction machines provide isolated strengthening of inner thigh muscles through controlled leg squeezing movements, making them ideal for beginners and rehabilitation settings.

## Key Points
- Targets the adductor muscle group (magnus, longus, brevis) responsible for bringing legs toward midline
- Machine-based design provides stable support and controlled resistance throughout range of motion
- Eccentric control during the return phase is crucial for muscle development and injury prevention
- Can be progressed through load increases, tempo variations, or advanced to free-weight alternatives
- Particularly beneficial for athletes requiring lateral stability and groin injury prevention

## Deep Dive

### Movement Mechanics
The hip adduction machine isolates the adductor muscles through a seated position that stabilizes the pelvis and spine. The movement involves bringing the legs together against resistance pads positioned on the outer thighs or knees. This creates pure hip adduction without compensation from other muscle groups, making it highly effective for targeted strengthening.

### Primary Muscle Activation
The exercise primarily targets five key adductor muscles: adductor magnus (the largest), adductor longus, adductor brevis, pectineus, and gracilis. The adductor magnus contributes significantly to hip stability and power transfer, while the gracilis also assists with knee flexion. Secondary activation occurs in the lower fibers of the gluteus maximus and core stabilizers.

### Training Applications
Machine hip adduction serves multiple training purposes. For beginners, it provides a safe introduction to adductor strengthening with built-in stability. In rehabilitation, it allows controlled loading of healing groin injuries. Athletes benefit from improved lateral movement stability and injury prevention, particularly in sports requiring rapid direction changes.

## Research Evidence

**Delmore, R.J., et al. (2014).** Adductor longus activation during common hip exercises. *Journal of Sport Rehabilitation*, 23(2), 79-87. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1123/jsr.2012-0046
- Key finding: Machine hip adduction produced significantly higher adductor longus activation compared to compound exercises
- Study type: Observational (EMG analysis)
- Evidence quality: Medium (controlled lab conditions, small sample size)

**Tyler, T.F., et al. (2001).** The role of hip muscle function in the treatment of patellofemoral pain syndrome. *American Journal of Sports Medicine*, 29(5), 579-585. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465010290050701
- Key finding: Hip adductor strengthening reduced patellofemoral pain and improved functional outcomes
- Study type: RCT
- Evidence quality: High (randomized controlled design, validated outcome measures)

**Holmich, P., et al. (1999).** Effectiveness of active physical training as treatment for long-standing adductor-related groin pain in athletes. *Lancet*, 353(9151), 439-443. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(98)06146-X
- Key finding: Progressive adductor strengthening successfully treated 79% of athletes with chronic groin pain
- Study type: RCT
- Evidence quality: High (well-designed trial with long-term follow-up)

## Practical Applications

**Setup Protocol:**
- Adjust machine so legs start in comfortable abducted position (typically 30-45 degrees)
- Set back support to maintain upright posture
- Position pads on outer thighs or knees based on comfort and machine design
- Start with light resistance to establish proper movement pattern

**Programming Guidelines:**
- Beginners: 2-3 sets of 12-15 reps with moderate resistance
- Strength focus: 3-4 sets of 6-10 reps with heavier load
- Endurance/rehabilitation: 2-3 sets of 15-20 reps with lighter resistance
- Frequency: 2-3 times per week with 48-hour recovery between sessions

**Progression Strategies:**
- Load progression: Increase weight by 5-10% when target reps achieved with good form
- Tempo variations: 3-second eccentric phase or 2-second pause at peak contraction
- Range of motion: Progress to fuller range as flexibility improves

## Common Mistakes
⚠️ Using momentum or bouncing at end ranges instead of controlled movement
⚠️ Incomplete range of motion, particularly rushing through the eccentric phase
⚠️ Lifting hips or shifting torso position, which reduces adductor isolation
⚠️ Starting with excessive weight that compromises form and control

## Practical Applications

**Exercise Parameters:**
- **Beginner Protocol:** 2-3 sets × 12-15 reps, 2-3x/week
- **Strength Development:** 3-4 sets × 6-10 reps with heavier resistance
- **Rehabilitation:** 2-3 sets × 15-20 reps with lighter load, focus on control

**Progression Sequence:**
1. Master bodyweight stability ball squeezes [[B19. Isometrics]]
2. Progress to machine hip adduction with light resistance
3. Increase load following [[B1. Progressive Overload]] principles
4. Advance to cable variations or Copenhagen planks

**Integration with Training:**
- Combine with hip abductor work for balanced development
- Use as warm-up for compound lower body exercises [[EXL-01 Barbell Back Squat (High Bar & Low Bar)]]
- Include in injury prevention programs for athletes in cutting sports
