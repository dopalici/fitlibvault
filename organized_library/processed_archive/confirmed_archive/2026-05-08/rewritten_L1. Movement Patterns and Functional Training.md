### L1. Movement Patterns and Functional Training
**ID:** L1-MOVEMENT-PATTERNS
**Created:** 2025-04-24 (Updated: 2025-01-27)

## Quick Takeaway
Functional training prioritizes movement patterns over isolated muscles, training the body to perform activities of daily living and sport-specific actions more efficiently and safely.

## Key Points
- Train the 7 fundamental movement patterns: squat, hinge, lunge, push, pull, carry, and rotate/anti-rotate
- Focus on multi-joint, multi-planar movements that mirror real-world activities
- Emphasize movement quality and control before adding load or complexity
- Progress systematically from bodyweight to loaded variations
- Integrate stability and mobility training for optimal function

## Deep Dive

Functional training represents a paradigm shift from traditional bodybuilding approaches, emphasizing the training of *movements* rather than isolated muscles. This approach focuses on patterns that directly transfer to activities of daily living (ADL) and sport-specific actions, improving movement quality, coordination, stability, and overall functional capacity.

### The Seven Fundamental Movement Patterns

Research suggests that most human movement can be categorized into fundamental patterns, though categorizations may vary among experts. The most commonly recognized patterns include:

#### 1. Squat Pattern
Characterized by dominant knee and hip flexion/extension in the vertical plane while maintaining an upright torso. This pattern involves coordinated movement of the ankles, knees, and hips.

**Examples:** Bodyweight squat, goblet squat, barbell back squat, front squat
**Real-world applications:** Sitting down and standing up, lifting objects from low surfaces, getting in/out of cars

#### 2. Hinge Pattern
Features dominant hip flexion and extension with minimal knee bend while maintaining a neutral spine. The movement is initiated from the hips with the torso hinging forward.

**Examples:** Deadlift variations, kettlebell swing, good morning, Romanian deadlift
**Real-world applications:** Picking objects off the floor, bending to tie shoes, jumping movements

#### 3. Lunge Pattern
Involves stepping patterns requiring single-leg stability combined with hip and knee flexion/extension. This pattern challenges unilateral strength and stability.

**Examples:** Forward lunge, reverse lunge, lateral lunge, step-ups, walking lunges
**Real-world applications:** Walking, running, climbing stairs, stepping over obstacles

#### 4. Push Pattern
Upper body pressing movements that can occur in horizontal or vertical planes.

**Horizontal Push:** Push-ups, bench press, chest press
**Vertical Push:** Overhead press, handstand push-ups, pike press
**Real-world applications:** Pushing objects away, getting up from the floor, placing items on high shelves

#### 5. Pull Pattern
Upper body pulling movements occurring in horizontal or vertical planes, counterbalancing push patterns.

**Horizontal Pull:** Bent-over rows, seated cable rows, inverted rows
**Vertical Pull:** Pull-ups, chin-ups, lat pulldowns
**Real-world applications:** Pulling objects toward the body, climbing, opening heavy doors

#### 6. Carry Pattern
Locomotion while carrying external loads, developing grip strength, core stability, and work capacity simultaneously.

**Examples:** Farmer's walk, suitcase carry, overhead carry, front-loaded carry
**Real-world applications:** Carrying groceries, luggage, moving boxes, carrying children

#### 7. Rotate/Anti-Rotation Pattern
Involves creating or resisting rotational forces through the torso and extremities.

**Rotation:** Medicine ball throws, woodchops, Russian twists
**Anti-Rotation:** Pallof press, single-arm carries, bird dog
**Real-world applications:** Throwing, swinging, stabilizing the torso during asymmetrical movements

This pattern closely relates to core stability training [[L4. Core Stability Training]].

### Core Principles of Functional Training

#### Multi-Joint and Multi-Planar Movement
Functional exercises typically involve multiple joints working in coordination across the three planes of motion: sagittal, frontal, and transverse planes. This approach better mirrors real-world movement demands compared to single-joint, single-plane exercises. Understanding movement analysis [[G-VII-2. Kinesiology Movement Analysis]] enhances exercise selection and progression.

#### Integrated Stability and Mobility
Effective functional training requires the coordinated control of joint mobility and stability. Areas requiring greater mobility (thoracic spine, hips, ankles) must be balanced with areas requiring stability (lumbar spine, core, scapulae). This integration is supported by targeted mobility work [[L2. Mobility and Flexibility Development]] and core stability training [[L4. Core Stability Training]].

#### Proprioceptive Enhancement
Functional training often incorporates free weights, bodyweight exercises, and carefully selected unstable surfaces to enhance proprioception and neuromuscular control. This approach improves body awareness and movement coordination, though unstable surface training should be used judiciously [[L3. Balance and Coordination Training]].

#### Movement Quality Over Quantity
Proper technique and movement control take precedence over lifting heavy weights or performing high repetitions. This principle aligns with proper lifting mechanics [[A18. Proper Lifting Mechanics]] and often begins with movement screening to identify baseline capabilities and limitations [[A17. Movement Screening]].

## Practical Applications

### Program Structure
- Begin each session with movement preparation focusing on mobility and activation
- Include 2-3 movement patterns per session, ensuring weekly coverage of all patterns
- Progress from bodyweight to external load systematically
- Integrate unilateral (single-limb) variations to address asymmetries

### Progression Framework
1. **Foundation Phase:** Master bodyweight versions with perfect form
2. **Load Phase:** Add external resistance while maintaining movement quality
3. **Integration Phase:** Combine patterns and add complexity (speed, direction changes)
4. **Specialization Phase:** Sport or activity-specific variations

### Sample Weekly Structure
- **Day 1:** Squat + Push + Carry patterns
- **Day 2:** Hinge + Pull + Anti-rotation patterns  
- **Day 3:** Lunge + Push/Pull + Rotation patterns

### Assessment and Progression Guidelines
- Use movement screens to identify starting points and limitations
- Progress load by 5-10% when 2-3 sets of 8-12 repetitions can be completed with perfect form
- Introduce single-limb variations after bilateral patterns are mastered
- Add unstable surfaces only when stable surface movements are proficient

## Common Mistakes

⚠️ **Progressing too quickly:** Adding load or complexity before mastering basic movement patterns leads to compensation and potential injury

⚠️ **Ignoring individual limitations:** Failing to address mobility restrictions or stability deficits through movement screening [[A17. Movement Screening]]

⚠️ **Overusing unstable surfaces:** Excessive instability training can reduce force production and movement quality without providing functional benefits

⚠️ **Neglecting unilateral training:** Focusing only on bilateral movements misses opportunities to address asymmetries and improve single-limb function

⚠️ **Poor exercise selection:** Choosing exercises that don't match individual goals, capabilities, or movement quality standards

⚠️ **Skipping movement preparation:** Jumping into loaded exercises without proper warm-up and movement activation
