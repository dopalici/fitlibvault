### I1. Training and Hormonal Responses
**ID:** I1-HORMONAL-RESPONSES
**Created:** 2025-04-24 (Updated: 2025-01-12)

## Quick Takeaway
Exercise triggers immediate hormonal changes that mobilize energy and promote adaptations, while consistent training improves insulin sensitivity and stress tolerance—but excessive training without adequate recovery can disrupt hormonal balance.

## Key Points
- High-intensity or long-duration exercise triggers significant increases in stress hormones like cortisol and adrenaline
- Resistance training with moderate-to-high volume stimulates growth hormone and testosterone release
- Regular exercise dramatically improves insulin sensitivity, requiring less insulin to manage blood sugar
- Overtraining combined with inadequate nutrition can suppress anabolic hormones and elevate chronic stress markers
- Trained athletes show blunted stress responses to the same absolute workload, indicating better physiological efficiency

## Deep Dive

### Acute Hormonal Responses (During and Post-Exercise)

Exercise acts as a controlled physiological stressor that triggers immediate hormonal cascades to meet energy demands and initiate repair processes. The magnitude and duration of these responses depend on exercise intensity, duration, and individual training status.

#### Catabolic Hormones: Fuel Mobilization and Stress Response

**Catecholamines (Epinephrine and Norepinephrine)**
These "fight-or-flight" hormones increase rapidly with exercise intensity and duration. They stimulate glycogenolysis (muscle glycogen breakdown), lipolysis (fat breakdown), and cardiovascular responses including increased heart rate, blood pressure, and cardiac contractility. The catecholamine response is proportional to exercise intensity and serves as the primary driver of acute metabolic adjustments.

**Cortisol**
Often called the "stress hormone," cortisol increases with moderate-to-high intensity exercise (>60% VO2max) or prolonged duration (>60-90 minutes). It stimulates gluconeogenesis (glucose production from non-carbohydrate sources), peripheral protein breakdown, and fat mobilization. While cortisol has beneficial anti-inflammatory effects acutely, chronically elevated levels become immunosuppressive and catabolic.

**Glucagon**
This hormone increases during exercise to counteract insulin's effects and stimulate hepatic glucose production, maintaining stable blood glucose levels during energy demands.

#### Anabolic and Growth-Related Hormones

**Growth Hormone (GH)**
GH shows pulsatile release that increases markedly with high-volume resistance training (metabolic stress) or moderate-intensity endurance exercise. It stimulates lipolysis and promotes protein synthesis indirectly through IGF-1 production. The GH response is particularly pronounced with exercises that create significant metabolic stress.

**Testosterone**
May increase transiently, particularly with heavy resistance training involving large muscle mass at moderate-to-high intensity and volume. However, responses are highly variable between individuals and can be blunted by excessive training volume or low energy availability.

**Insulin-like Growth Factor 1 (IGF-1)**
This hormone mediates many of GH's effects and plays crucial roles in muscle growth and repair. IGF-1 levels can increase post-exercise, supporting the anabolic response to training stimuli.

#### Other Important Hormones

**Insulin**
Insulin levels typically decrease during exercise as catecholamines inhibit its release, allowing for fuel mobilization. Critically, insulin sensitivity increases post-exercise, enhancing glucose uptake efficiency for hours to days following training.

**Endorphins**
These "feel-good" hormones increase during exercise, contributing to pain relief and mood improvements associated with physical activity.

### Chronic Adaptations from Regular Training

#### Improved Insulin Sensitivity
Regular exercise—both aerobic and resistance training—produces dramatic improvements in insulin sensitivity. This allows muscles to take up glucose more efficiently with less insulin required, representing one of exercise's most important metabolic benefits and a key factor in metabolic health.

#### Altered Basal Hormone Levels
Moderate training typically produces minimal changes in resting hormone levels. However, chronic high-volume/intensity training, especially when combined with low energy availability (see [[I3. Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S)]]), can lead to problematic adaptations:

- **Suppressed Anabolic Hormones:** Lower resting testosterone in males and menstrual dysfunction/low estrogen in females
- **Elevated Resting Cortisol:** Indicating chronic stress and inadequate recovery

#### Attenuated Exercise Responses
Well-trained individuals often show blunted catecholamine and cortisol responses to the same absolute exercise workload, reflecting improved physiological efficiency and stress tolerance. This adaptation allows athletes to handle higher training loads with less physiological disruption.

## Research Evidence

**Hackney, A.C. & Lane, A.R. (2015).** Exercise and the regulation of endocrine hormones. *Progress in Molecular Biology and Translational Science*, 135, 293-311. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/bs.pmbts.2015.07.001
- Key finding: Exercise intensity above 60% VO2max consistently triggers significant cortisol and catecholamine responses
- Study type: Systematic review
- Evidence quality: High (comprehensive analysis of endocrine responses across exercise modalities)

**Stellingwerff, T. & Cox, G.R. (2014).** Systematic review: Carbohydrate supplementation on exercise performance or capacity of varying durations. *Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism*, 39(9), 998-1011. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2014-0027
- Key finding: Carbohydrate availability significantly modulates cortisol responses and maintains anabolic hormone balance during prolonged exercise
- Study type: Systematic review
- Evidence quality: High (meta-analysis of nutritional interventions on hormonal responses)

**Cadegiani, F.A. & Kater, C.E. (2017).** Hormonal aspects of overtraining syndrome: a systematic review. *BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation*, 9, 14. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-017-0079-8
- Key finding: Overtraining syndrome consistently associated with suppressed testosterone, elevated cortisol, and disrupted growth hormone patterns
- Study type: Systematic review
- Evidence quality: High (comprehensive analysis of overtraining biomarkers)

## Practical Applications

**Optimizing Hormonal Responses:**
- Schedule high-intensity sessions when cortisol is naturally higher (morning) to align with circadian rhythms
- Use resistance training with 6-12 reps, multiple sets, and short rest periods (30-90 seconds) to maximize GH response
- Allow 48-72 hours between intense sessions targeting the same muscle groups to optimize anabolic hormone recovery

**Monitoring Training Stress:**
- Track resting heart rate, sleep quality, and mood as indirect markers of hormonal balance
- Consider morning cortisol testing during high-volume training phases
- Monitor performance metrics—declining power/strength may indicate hormonal disruption

**Nutritional Support:**
- Maintain adequate energy intake to support hormonal function, especially during intensive training phases
- Consume post-workout nutrition within 30-60 minutes to optimize insulin sensitivity and anabolic responses
- Ensure adequate carbohydrate availability during prolonged or high-intensity sessions to minimize excessive cortisol elevation

## Common Mistakes

⚠️ **Assuming more training always equals better hormonal adaptations**—excessive volume without adequate recovery disrupts the anabolic-catabolic balance

⚠️ **Ignoring the role of energy availability**—inadequate nutrition significantly blunts beneficial hormonal responses regardless of training quality

⚠️ **Focusing solely on acute responses**—chronic adaptations like improved insulin sensitivity are often more important than immediate post-exercise hormone spikes

⚠️ **Overlooking individual variability**—hormonal responses vary significantly between individuals based on genetics, training history, and lifestyle factors

⚠️ **Training through persistent fatigue**—chronic elevation of stress hormones without adequate recovery can lead to overtraining syndrome
