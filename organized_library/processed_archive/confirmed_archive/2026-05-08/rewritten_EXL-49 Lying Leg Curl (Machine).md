### **EXL-49: Lying Leg Curl (Machine)**

**ID:** EXL-49-MEX-LEGCURL-LYING **Created:** 2025-04-30 (Updated: 2025-01-14)

## Quick Takeaway
The lying leg curl is a machine-based isolation exercise that targets the hamstrings while allowing for greater hip extension compared to seated variations, making it effective for hamstring development and knee flexion strength.

## Key Points
- Targets all three hamstring muscles (biceps femoris, semitendinosus, semimembranosus) through knee flexion
- Prone position allows for slight hip extension, which can enhance hamstring activation
- Machine provides stable resistance curve and controlled movement pattern
- Allows for progressive overload through weight, reps, and tempo modifications
- Suitable for beginners due to machine stability and guided movement path

## Deep Dive

### Movement Mechanics
The lying leg curl is performed in a prone position on a specialized machine [[B10. Machine Training]]. The exercise involves knee flexion against resistance provided by a lever arm system. The prone position allows the hips to maintain slight extension, which can enhance hamstring activation compared to seated variations where hip flexion may limit hamstring recruitment.

### Primary Muscle Activation
**Target Muscles:**
- **Primary Movers (Agonists):** Hamstrings (Biceps Femoris - short & long heads, Semitendinosus, Semimembranosus)
- **Secondary Movers (Synergists):** Gastrocnemius (assists knee flexion), Popliteus, Sartorius, Gracilis
- **Stabilizers:** Core musculature [[L4. Core Stability Training]], Glutes, Erector Spinae (to maintain prone position)

### Exercise Execution
**Setup and Form:** [[A18. Proper Lifting Mechanics]]
- Lie face down (prone) on the machine pad, aligning knees just off the edge of the pad and near the machine's axis of rotation
- Place the back of the lower legs/ankles securely under the roller pad
- Grip handles in front for stability and keep hips pressed into the pad
- Maintain neutral spine alignment throughout the movement

**Movement Pattern:**
- Exhale and flex knees, curling the roller pad towards glutes smoothly
- Focus on contracting the hamstrings while avoiding excessive hip lifting
- Pause briefly at peak contraction for enhanced muscle activation
- Inhale and slowly lower the pad back to starting position under control (eccentric phase)

### Common Technical Errors
- **Hip Elevation:** Lifting hips excessively off pad reduces hamstring activation and can stress the lower back
- **Momentum Use:** Swinging legs or using rapid movements reduces muscle tension and increases injury risk
- **Incomplete Range of Motion:** Not achieving full knee flexion or extension limits muscle development
- **Uncontrolled Eccentric:** Dropping the weight rapidly misses the strength-building benefits of the lowering phase

## Practical Applications

**Programming Guidelines:**
- **Beginner:** 2-3 sets of 10-15 reps at moderate resistance
- **Intermediate:** 3-4 sets of 8-12 reps with progressive load increases
- **Advanced:** 4-5 sets with varied rep ranges (6-15) and tempo manipulations

**Progression Strategies:**
- Increase load (weight stack) following [[B1. Progressive Overload]] principles
- Increase volume through reps/sets [[B5. Training Volume and Frequency]]
- Implement tempo variations (2-second pause at peak contraction, 3-second eccentric [[B20. Eccentric Training]])
- Progress to single-leg variations for unilateral strength development

**Integration with Other Exercises:**
- Use as accessory work following compound movements like [[EXL-09 Barbell Romanian Deadlift (RDL)]] or [[EXL-37 Dumbbell Romanian Deadlift (RDL)]]
- Pair with quadriceps exercises for balanced leg development
- Include in rehabilitation programs for hamstring strengthening

## Common Mistakes
⚠️ Lifting hips off the pad during the curl, which reduces hamstring activation and can stress the lower back
⚠️ Using momentum or swinging the legs instead of controlled muscle contraction
⚠️ Setting the machine incorrectly so knees don't align with the axis of rotation
⚠️ Rushing through the eccentric (lowering) phase, missing strength-building benefits
