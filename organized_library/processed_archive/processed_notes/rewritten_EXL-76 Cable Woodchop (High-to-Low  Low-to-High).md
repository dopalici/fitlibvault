### **EXL-76: Cable Woodchop (High-to-Low / Low-to-High)**

**ID:** EXL-76-CEX-WOODCHOP **Created:** 2025-04-30 (Updated: 2025-01-14)

## Quick Takeaway
A rotational core exercise using cables that builds functional strength for sports and daily activities requiring twisting motions, targeting the obliques through diagonal chopping movements.

## Key Points
- Start with light weight (5-15 lbs) to master the rotational movement pattern before progressing
- Keep arms relatively straight and initiate movement from your core, not your arms
- High-to-low emphasizes obliques differently than low-to-high - use both variations for complete development
- Progress by increasing weight, reps, or adding stability challenges (half-kneeling stance)
- Essential for athletes in rotational sports (golf, tennis, baseball) and functional daily movements

## Deep Dive

### Movement Description
A rotational core exercise using a cable machine [[B10. Machine Training]], involving pulling a handle diagonally across the body, either from a high anchor point downwards (high-to-low) or a low anchor point upwards (low-to-high). This exercise targets the obliques and other core muscles involved in rotation and anti-rotation [[L4. Core Stability Training]].

**Difficulty Level:** Intermediate (Requires good core control and coordination).

### Target Muscles
- **Primary Movers (Agonists):** Obliques (Internal and External), Rectus Abdominis. (Emphasis differs slightly between high-low and low-high variations)
- **Secondary Movers (Synergists):** Serratus Anterior, Hip Rotators, Shoulders (Deltoids), Arms (assist in controlling handle)
- **Stabilizers:** Transverse Abdominis, Glutes, Erector Spinae, Muscles of the legs/feet providing base of support [[L4. Core Stability Training]]

### Proper Form and Technique [[A18. Proper Lifting Mechanics]]

**Setup (High-to-Low):**
- Set pulley to high position
- Stand sideways to machine, feet shoulder-width apart in stable stance
- Grasp handle (rope or D-handle) with both hands, arms mostly straight

**Setup (Low-to-High):**
- Set pulley to low position
- Stand sideways to machine in stable stance
- Grasp handle with both hands

**Execution (High-to-Low):**
- Keep arms relatively straight throughout movement
- Rotate torso diagonally downwards across body, pulling handle towards opposite knee/hip
- Pivot on back foot as needed
- Movement should be driven by core rotation, not just arms
- Control the return path slowly

**Execution (Low-to-High):**
- Keep arms relatively straight throughout movement
- Rotate torso diagonally upwards across body, pulling handle towards opposite shoulder
- Pivot on back foot as needed
- Control the return path with same tempo as pulling phase

**Breathing Pattern:**
- Exhale during chopping motion (concentric phase)
- Inhale during return phase (eccentric phase)

### Progression Pathways

**Advancement Options:**
- Increase load (weight stack) following [[B1. Progressive Overload]] principles
- Increase reps/sets according to [[B5. Training Volume and Frequency]] guidelines
- Tempo variations (controlled rotation, slow eccentric [[B20. Eccentric Training]])
- Perform in half-kneeling or tall-kneeling stance (increases stability challenge)

**Regression Options:**
- Decrease load/resistance
- Reduce range of motion (less rotation)
- Pallof Press [[EXL-77 Cable Pallof Press (Anti-Rotation)]] (focuses on anti-rotation, less dynamic)
- Resistance band woodchops [[B11. Resistance Bands]]
- Bodyweight Russian twists [[EXL-90 Russian Twist (Bodyweight or Weighted)]]

## Research Evidence

Research on rotational core training demonstrates significant benefits for functional movement and athletic performance. Studies show that rotational exercises like woodchops activate the obliques more effectively than traditional crunches, with EMG studies indicating higher muscle activation in the external obliques during diagonal movement patterns (Ekstrom et al., 2007).

Cable-based rotational exercises have been shown to improve rotational power output in athletes, particularly benefiting sports requiring transverse plane movement (Hibbs et al., 2008). The functional carryover to athletic performance is well-documented, with improvements in golf swing velocity and baseball throwing speed following rotational training programs.

## Practical Applications

**Training Parameters:**
- **Beginners:** 2-3 sets of 8-12 reps per side, 5-15 lbs
- **Intermediate:** 3-4 sets of 10-15 reps per side, 15-30 lbs
- **Advanced:** 3-5 sets of 12-20 reps per side, 30+ lbs or stability challenges

**Sport-Specific Applications:**
- **Golf:** High-to-low mimics downswing mechanics
- **Tennis:** Both variations improve serve and groundstroke power
- **Baseball/Softball:** Enhances batting and throwing mechanics
- **General Fitness:** Improves functional movement for lifting and carrying objects

**Programming Recommendations:**
- Include both high-to-low and low-to-high variations in training cycles
- Perform 2-3 times per week as part of core training routine
- Can be used as activation exercise or primary strength movement

## Common Mistakes

⚠️ **Pulling primarily with arms instead of rotating from the core** - This reduces effectiveness and can strain shoulders

⚠️ **Using excessive momentum or bouncing movements** - Compromises control and increases injury risk

⚠️ **Insufficient core rotation/engagement** - Limits the functional benefit of the exercise

⚠️ **Losing neutral spine posture during movement** - Can stress the lower back inappropriately

⚠️ **Improper foot positioning or pivoting** - Reduces stability and power transfer

⚠️ **Starting with too much weight** - Master the movement pattern first before adding significant resistance

## Contraindications & Safety

**Medical Conditions Requiring Caution:**
- Acute low back pain (especially rotation-sensitive conditions like disc issues) [[IX-6. Exercise Contraindications]]
- Shoulder injuries or impingement
- Recent abdominal or core muscle injuries

**Safety Guidelines:**
- Start with light weight to master rotational control
- Maintain neutral spine throughout movement
- Focus on controlled rotation initiated from core muscles
- Ensure stable footing and proper machine setup
- Progress weight gradually following [[B1. Progressive Overload]] principles
