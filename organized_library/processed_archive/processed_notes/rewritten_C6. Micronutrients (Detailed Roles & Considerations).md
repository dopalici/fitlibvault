### C6. Micronutrients (Detailed)
**ID:** C6-MICRONUTRIENTS-DETAILED
**Created:** 2025-04-26 (Updated: 2025-01-27)

## Quick Takeaway
Active individuals have higher micronutrient needs than sedentary people, with vitamin D, iron, and B12 being the most commonly deficient nutrients that directly impact performance and recovery.

## Key Points
- Blood tests for vitamin D and iron status are essential for athletes and highly active individuals
- Whole foods provide better micronutrient absorption than supplements for most nutrients
- Female, endurance, and vegetarian/vegan athletes face highest risk for iron and B12 deficiencies
- Vitamin D deficiency affects up to 50% of athletes regardless of diet quality
- High-dose antioxidant supplements can interfere with beneficial training adaptations

## Deep Dive

This note expands on [[A4. Micronutrients]], detailing specific vitamins and minerals critical for exercise performance, recovery, and overall health. While balanced diets often meet general population needs, athletes may have increased requirements or specific risks due to higher turnover, losses through sweat, or dietary restrictions.

### Priority Micronutrients for Active Individuals

**Vitamin D - The Foundation Nutrient**

Vitamin D functions as both a vitamin and hormone, with receptors present throughout the body including skeletal muscle tissue. It regulates calcium absorption for bone health [[G-VII-1. Musculoskeletal Anatomy Overview]], supports immune function, and enables proper muscle contraction.

Food sources include fatty fish (salmon, mackerel), fortified dairy products, egg yolks, and fortified cereals. However, even optimal diets rarely provide sufficient amounts, as most vitamin D comes from sun exposure.

Deficiency affects up to 50% of athletes globally, with higher risk for indoor athletes, those training at higher latitudes, individuals with darker skin, and during winter months. Low vitamin D status impairs muscle function, compromises bone health, and may increase upper respiratory infection risk.

**Iron - The Performance Limiter**

Iron serves as the core component of hemoglobin for oxygen transport and myoglobin for muscle oxygen storage. It's essential for aerobic energy production [[A6. Energy Systems (Detailed)]] and cellular respiration.

Heme iron from animal sources (red meat, poultry, fish) has high bioavailability. Non-heme iron from plant sources (legumes, fortified grains, leafy greens, tofu) has lower absorption rates, especially when consumed with phytates or calcium.

Iron deficiency progresses through three distinct stages: iron depletion (low ferritin stores), iron-deficient erythropoiesis (impaired red blood cell production), and iron-deficiency anemia (low hemoglobin). Each stage progressively impairs exercise capacity, with anemia severely limiting endurance performance.

Female, endurance, and vegetarian/vegan athletes face highest deficiency risk due to menstrual losses, increased exercise demands, and reliance on less bioavailable plant sources.

### Essential Supporting Nutrients

**B Vitamins Complex**

B vitamins function as coenzymes in energy metabolism pathways. Thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, and B6 are widely available in varied diets through whole grains, lean proteins, and vegetables.

**Vitamin B12 requires special attention** as it's found almost exclusively in animal products. Deficiency develops slowly over years but causes irreversible neurological damage. Vegetarian and vegan athletes [[C11. Vegetarian & Vegan Diets]] must supplement.

**Calcium and Magnesium**

Calcium supports bone structure and muscle contraction, available through dairy products, fortified plant milks, and leafy greens. Particularly critical for athletes at risk of low energy availability [[I3. Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S)]] and female athletes [[I2. Female-Specific Physiological Considerations]].

Magnesium functions as a cofactor in over 300 enzymatic reactions including ATP synthesis and protein metabolism. Found in nuts, seeds, whole grains, and leafy greens. Exercise increases sweat losses, potentially elevating needs.

**Antioxidant Nutrients**

Vitamins C and E, along with selenium, protect against exercise-induced oxidative stress [[C34. Antioxidants]]. However, high-dose supplementation may blunt beneficial training adaptations by interfering with cellular signaling pathways that promote adaptation.

## Practical Applications

**Annual Testing Protocol:**
- Vitamin D (25(OH)D): Target >75 nmol/L (30 ng/mL)
- Iron status (ferritin): Women >30 ng/mL, Men >40 ng/mL
- B12 if vegetarian/vegan: Target >300 pg/mL
- Complete metabolic panel including magnesium, zinc

**Evidence-Based Supplementation:**
- Vitamin D: 1000-4000 IU daily (adjust based on blood levels and season)
- Iron: Only when deficient, 18-65mg with vitamin C for absorption
- B12 for vegans: 250-500 mcg daily or 2500 mcg weekly
- Magnesium: 200-400mg if dietary intake inadequate

**Food-First Strategy:**
Prioritize nutrient-dense whole foods [[C30. Food Quality & Health (Whole vs Processed)]]: colorful vegetables and fruits, lean proteins, whole grains, nuts, and seeds provide superior bioavailability compared to synthetic supplements.

## Common Mistakes

⚠️ **Taking iron supplements without testing** - Can cause gastrointestinal distress, toxicity, and interfere with zinc and calcium absorption

⚠️ **Megadose antioxidant supplementation** - High doses of vitamins C and E may blunt beneficial training adaptations and cellular signaling

⚠️ **Ignoring B12 as a vegetarian/vegan** - Deficiency develops over 2-5 years but causes irreversible neurological damage

⚠️ **Assuming diet alone provides adequate vitamin D** - Even fish-rich diets rarely provide sufficient amounts for optimal status

⚠️ **Self-diagnosing deficiencies based on symptoms** - Fatigue, weakness, and poor recovery have many causes; blood testing is essential for accurate diagnosis
