### A6. Energy Systems (Detailed)
**ID:** A6-ENERGY-SYSTEMS
**Created:** 2025-04-23 (Updated: 2026-05-06)

## Quick Takeaway
Your body uses three energy systems simultaneously to fuel exercise, with dominance shifting based on intensity and duration - understanding this helps optimize training and performance.

## Key Points
- **ATP-PCr system** powers explosive movements (0-15 seconds) - think sprints, heavy lifts, jumps
- **Glycolytic system** fuels high-intensity efforts (15 seconds to 2-3 minutes) - like 400m runs or HIIT intervals
- **Oxidative system** sustains prolonged activities (3+ minutes) - endurance running, cycling, swimming
- All three systems work together simultaneously, but training can enhance specific systems
- Recovery between efforts allows systems to replenish and continue contributing

## Deep Dive

The body uses three primary metabolic pathways to regenerate ATP (adenosine triphosphate), the immediate energy currency for muscle contraction. These systems operate concurrently, with their relative contributions determined by exercise intensity and duration.

### ATP-PCr System (Phosphagen System)
- **Fuel Source:** Stored ATP and Phosphocreatine (PCr) in muscle
- **Mechanism:** Creatine Kinase enzyme rapidly transfers phosphate from PCr to ADP, regenerating ATP
- **Characteristics:** 
  - Completely anaerobic (no oxygen required)
  - Highest power output of all systems
  - Very limited capacity (~5-15 seconds of maximal effort)
  - No fatiguing metabolic byproducts
- **Recovery Profile:** PCr replenishes rapidly during rest with ~30-60 second half-life
- **Training Applications:** Explosive power development, Olympic lifts, sprints

### Glycolytic System (Anaerobic Glycolysis)
- **Fuel Source:** Glucose from blood and muscle glycogen
- **Mechanism:** Glucose/glycogen breakdown to pyruvate, then converted to lactate to regenerate NAD+ for continued glycolysis
- **Characteristics:**
  - Anaerobic process
  - High power output (less than ATP-PCr but greater than oxidative)
  - Limited capacity (~15 seconds to 2-3 minutes of high-intensity work)
  - Produces lactate and H+ ions that contribute to muscular fatigue
- **Training Applications:** High-intensity intervals, middle-distance events, repeated sprint training

### Oxidative System (Aerobic System)
- **Fuel Sources:** Pyruvate (from glucose), fatty acids, amino acids (minor contribution)
- **Mechanism:** Complete substrate oxidation through mitochondrial Krebs Cycle and Electron Transport Chain
- **Characteristics:**
  - Requires oxygen
  - Lower power output compared to anaerobic systems
  - Virtually unlimited capacity (limited primarily by fuel availability and heat dissipation)
  - Primary energy system at rest and during submaximal, prolonged exercise
  - Most efficient ATP production per substrate molecule
- **Training Applications:** Endurance sports, aerobic base building, recovery between high-intensity efforts

### System Interactions
All energy systems contribute simultaneously during exercise, but their relative contributions shift based on intensity and duration. The crossover between systems isn't absolute but rather a continuum where one system gradually becomes more dominant.

## Research Evidence

**Gastin PB (2001).** Energy system interaction and relative contribution during maximal exercise. *Sports Medicine*, 31(10), 725-741. PMID: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11547894
- Key finding: Demonstrated overlapping contributions of energy systems with clear intensity-duration relationships showing ATP-PCr dominance in first 10 seconds, glycolytic peak at 1-2 minutes, and oxidative system increasing contribution over time
- Study type: Systematic review
- Evidence quality: High (comprehensive analysis of multiple metabolic studies)

**Spencer MR, Gastin PB (2001).** Energy system contribution during 200- to 1500-m running in highly trained athletes. *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, 33(1), 157-162. PMID: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11194103
- Key finding: Quantified energy system contributions showing 200m race is 29% aerobic/71% anaerobic while 1500m is 86% aerobic/14% anaerobic
- Study type: Observational study
- Evidence quality: High (direct measurement in elite athletes using validated methods)

**Bogdanis GC et al. (1996).** Contribution of phosphocreatine and aerobic metabolism to energy supply during repeated sprint exercise. *Journal of Applied Physiology*, 80(3), 876-884. PMID: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8964751
- Key finding: PCr system provides 50% of energy in first 6-second sprint but only 25% in subsequent sprints, with aerobic contribution increasing with each repetition
- Study type: Experimental study
- Evidence quality: High (controlled laboratory conditions with muscle biopsy validation)

## Practical Applications

**Training Zone Targeting:**
- **Power/Strength:** 1-6 reps, 3-5 minutes rest (ATP-PCr focus)
- **Power Endurance:** 15-45 second intervals, 1:3 work-to-rest ratio (glycolytic focus)
- **Aerobic Base:** 60-180+ minute continuous efforts at conversational pace (oxidative focus)

**Sport-Specific Applications:**
- **Sprinters:** Emphasize ATP-PCr through short, explosive efforts with full recovery
- **Middle-distance athletes:** Target glycolytic system with lactate threshold and VO2max intervals
- **Endurance athletes:** Build oxidative capacity through high-volume, moderate-intensity training

**Recovery Optimization:**
- Allow 2-3 minutes between high-intensity efforts for PCr replenishment
- Use active recovery (light movement) to enhance lactate clearance
- Plan easy days after high glycolytic stress to allow metabolic recovery

## Common Mistakes

⚠️ **Assuming systems work in isolation** - All three systems always contribute; training should reflect this reality

⚠️ **Inadequate recovery between power efforts** - Rushing sets before PCr replenishment limits power output and training adaptation

⚠️ **Ignoring fuel requirements** - Glycolytic and oxidative systems require adequate carbohydrate availability for optimal function

⚠️ **Training the wrong system for sport demands** - Match training intensity/duration to competitive requirements

⚠️ **Neglecting system interactions** - Elite performance requires all systems to be well-developed, even if one is primary
