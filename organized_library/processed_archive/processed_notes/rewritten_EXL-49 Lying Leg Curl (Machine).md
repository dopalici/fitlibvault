### **EXL-49: Lying Leg Curl (Machine)**

**ID:** EXL-49-MEX-LEGCURL-LYING 
**Created:** 2025-04-30 (Updated: 2025-01-27)

## Quick Takeaway
A beginner-friendly isolation exercise that targets the hamstrings while lying face-down on a machine, offering better hip positioning than seated versions for maximum hamstring activation.

## Key Points
- Lie prone with knees just off the pad edge and ankles under the roller
- Keep hips pressed down throughout the movement to maintain proper alignment
- Focus on controlled tempo with a brief pause at peak contraction
- Start with bodyweight resistance bands if the machine feels too heavy
- Progress by adding weight, reps, or tempo variations before moving to free weights

## Deep Dive

### Exercise Mechanics and Setup
The lying leg curl is performed on a specialized machine [[B10. Machine Training]] in a prone (face-down) position. This positioning allows for slight hip extension, which can enhance hamstring activation compared to seated variations. The exercise involves knee flexion against resistance provided by a lever arm system.

Proper setup requires aligning the knees just off the edge of the machine pad, near the axis of rotation. The roller pad should contact the back of the lower legs/ankles, with hips pressed firmly into the pad throughout the movement.

### Target Muscle Activation
The primary movers are the hamstring complex: biceps femoris (both short and long heads), semitendinosus, and semimembranosus. Secondary assistance comes from the gastrocnemius (which crosses the knee joint), popliteus, sartorius, and gracilis. Core musculature [[L4. Core Stability Training]], glutes, and erector spinae act as stabilizers to maintain the prone position.

### Execution Technique
Following proper lifting mechanics [[A18. Proper Lifting Mechanics]], the movement begins with knee flexion while exhaling, curling the roller pad smoothly toward the glutes. The key is maintaining hip contact with the pad while focusing on hamstring contraction. A brief pause at peak contraction enhances muscle activation before the controlled eccentric lowering phase.

## Research Evidence
Research supports isolation exercises like leg curls for targeted hamstring development, particularly when combined with compound movements. Studies indicate that prone positioning may allow for greater hip extension compared to seated variations, potentially enhancing long head biceps femoris activation. However, specific comparative studies between lying and seated leg curl variations show mixed results regarding muscle activation differences.

## Practical Applications

### Programming Recommendations
- **Beginner:** 2-3 sets of 12-15 reps with light resistance
- **Intermediate:** 3-4 sets of 8-12 reps with moderate load
- **Advanced:** Include tempo variations (3-second eccentric) or single-leg variations

### Progression Strategy
Following progressive overload principles [[B1. Progressive Overload]], advance through:
1. Increased load via weight stack adjustment
2. Higher volume through additional reps/sets [[B5. Training Volume and Frequency]]
3. Tempo manipulations including eccentric emphasis [[B20. Eccentric Training]]
4. Unilateral (single-leg) variations
5. Transition to compound movements like Romanian deadlifts [[EXL-09 Barbell Romanian Deadlift (RDL)]] or [[EXL-37 Dumbbell Romanian Deadlift (RDL)]]

### Regression Options
For those unable to perform the machine version:
- Resistance band hamstring curls [[B11. Resistance Bands]]
- Stability ball hamstring curls [[B7. Bodyweight Training]]
- Basic glute bridges [[B7. Bodyweight Training]]

## Common Mistakes
⚠️ **Lifting hips off the pad** - This reduces hamstring activation and can stress the lower back
⚠️ **Using momentum or swinging** - Defeats the purpose of controlled isolation work
⚠️ **Incomplete range of motion** - Limits muscle development and flexibility benefits
⚠️ **Uncontrolled eccentric phase** - Wastes the muscle-building potential of the lowering portion
⚠️ **Hyperextending knees at bottom** - Can cause joint stress and injury
