### A28. Advanced Movement Assessment Methodologies
**ID:** A28
**Created:** [Original date] (Updated: December 2024)

## Quick Takeaway
Advanced movement assessment goes beyond basic screening to use quantitative measures, region-specific testing, and clinical reasoning to identify the root causes of movement dysfunction in rehabilitation and high-performance settings.

## Key Points
- Regional interdependence means dysfunction in one body area can cause problems in distant regions
- Movement impairment syndromes classify dysfunction based on faulty movement patterns and underlying muscle imbalances
- Clinical reasoning integrates assessment findings with patient history and biomechanical knowledge
- Technology-based assessments provide objective data but require specialized training and equipment
- Advanced methods focus on identifying specific causes rather than just gross movement limitations

## Deep Dive

### Theoretical Foundations

**Regional Interdependence** forms the cornerstone of advanced movement assessment. This concept recognizes that dysfunction in one body region can influence pain or performance in another area. For example, limited hip mobility can contribute to low back pain during squats, even though the pain is felt in the spine rather than the hip.

**Movement Impairment Syndromes**, developed by Sahrmann, provide a systematic approach to classifying movement dysfunction. These syndromes are based on the direction of faulty movement or alignment during specific tests, suggesting underlying muscle imbalances or stiffness patterns that require targeted intervention.

**Clinical Reasoning** distinguishes advanced assessment from basic screening. This process integrates assessment findings with patient history, symptom behavior, and knowledge of anatomy and biomechanics to form hypotheses about the root cause of movement issues.

### Assessment Methodologies

**Selective Functional Movement Assessment (SFMA™)** represents a clinical assessment system specifically designed for individuals with pain. Unlike basic screening tools, SFMA uses a top-tier set of movements to identify painful patterns, then systematically breaks down dysfunctional non-painful patterns into specific mobility or stability/motor control deficits using standardized tests.

**Systematic Regional Examination** involves detailed assessment of specific joints or regions identified as problematic during initial screening or patient history. This might include measuring specific shoulder internal/external rotation range of motion and strength, hip flexor length tests, or ankle dorsiflexion assessments.

**Dynamic Movement Analysis** observes movement quality during complex or sport-specific tasks such as jumping, landing, cutting, or throwing. This approach identifies compensatory patterns that emerge under load or fatigue conditions. Tools like the Landing Error Scoring System (LESS) provide quantitative measures of landing mechanics.

### Quantitative Analysis Technologies

**3D Motion Capture** serves as the gold standard for detailed kinematic analysis in research and elite performance settings. This technology provides precise measurements of joint angles, velocities, and movement patterns during complex tasks.

**Force Plates** measure ground reaction forces during activities like jumping, landing, walking, and running, connecting to [[A16. Biomechanics Gait Analysis]] principles. These devices reveal how forces are generated and absorbed during movement.

**Electromyography (EMG)** assesses muscle activation patterns and timing, linking to [[../documentation/G-VII-3. Neurophysiology of Movement]] concepts. This technology identifies when muscles are active and how they coordinate during movement tasks.

**Wearable Sensors (IMUs)** are increasingly used for field-based assessment of movement quantity and quality. While promising for practical applications and connecting to [[../equipment_technology/VIII-3. Wearable Technology (Fitness Trackers, Smartwatches, HRMs)]], their validity for specific clinical interpretations continues to evolve.

## Research Evidence

**Cook, G., Burton, L., & Hoogenboom, B. J. (2006).** Functional movement screening: the use of fundamental movements as an assessment of function-part 1. *North American Journal of Sports Physical Therapy*, 1(2), 62-72. PMID: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21522216
- Key finding: Established foundational principles for movement screening methodology
- Study type: Clinical methodology paper
- Evidence quality: Medium (foundational methodology without predictive validation)

**Glaws, K. R., et al. (2014).** The Selective Functional Movement Assessment (SFMA): Test-Retest and Interrater Reliability in a Sample of Active Adults. *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research*, 28(10), 2783-2791. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000000448
- Key finding: SFMA demonstrated good to excellent reliability (ICC = 0.74-0.95) among trained clinicians
- Study type: Reliability study
- Evidence quality: Medium (establishes reliability but not predictive validity)

**Bonazza, N. A., et al. (2017).** Reliability, validity, and injury predictive value of the functional movement screen: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *American Journal of Sports Medicine*, 45(3), 725-732. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546516641937
- Key finding: FMS showed moderate reliability but limited predictive validity for injury (pooled sensitivity 0.54, specificity 0.53)
- Study type: Systematic review and meta-analysis
- Evidence quality: High (comprehensive analysis of multiple studies)

## Practical Applications

**Clinical Implementation:**
- Use SFMA for patients with existing pain to identify movement patterns that reproduce symptoms
- Apply regional examination when basic screening identifies specific areas of concern
- Implement dynamic analysis for athletes returning from injury or seeking performance enhancement

**Technology Integration:**
- Reserve 3D motion capture for research settings or elite athlete assessment
- Use force plates for objective landing and jumping assessments
- Consider wearable sensors for ongoing movement monitoring in field settings

**Assessment Progression:**
1. Start with basic movement screening [[A17. Movement Screening]]
2. Identify areas requiring deeper investigation
3. Apply region-specific tests based on findings
4. Use technology-based assessment when objective data is needed
5. Integrate findings with clinical reasoning for intervention planning

## Common Mistakes

⚠️ **Over-relying on technology without clinical context** - Advanced tools provide data but require expert interpretation

⚠️ **Assuming perfect movement is always necessary** - Focus should be on functional improvement rather than achieving theoretical ideals

⚠️ **Ignoring pain science principles** - Movement dysfunction doesn't always directly correlate with pain or injury risk [[../health_conditions/IX-7. Pain Science in Exercise & Rehabilitation]]

⚠️ **Using advanced assessment without proper training** - Many methodologies require specific certification and clinical expertise

⚠️ **Failing to consider load management** - Movement quality under fatigue or high-load conditions may differ significantly from controlled assessment conditions
