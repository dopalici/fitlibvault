### A43. Compensatory Movement Patterns
**ID:** A43-COMPENSATORY-MOVEMENT-PATTERNS
**Created:** 2025-04-26 (Updated: 2025-01-14)

## Quick Takeaway
Compensatory movement patterns are alternative movement strategies the body adopts when normal movement is restricted, which can lead to inefficient movement and increased injury risk if not properly addressed.

## Key Points
- Compensatory patterns develop when the body works around mobility restrictions, strength imbalances, or previous injuries
- These patterns often involve recruiting different muscle groups or changing joint mechanics to accomplish movement tasks
- Common compensations include hip dominance during squats when ankle mobility is limited, or shoulder elevation when thoracic spine mobility is restricted
- Early identification through movement screening can prevent these patterns from becoming habitual and problematic
- Addressing root causes (mobility, stability, strength deficits) is more effective than just correcting the compensation itself

## Deep Dive

### Understanding Compensation Mechanisms

Compensatory movement patterns emerge as the nervous system's adaptive response to physical limitations or dysfunction. When the optimal movement pathway is blocked—whether by joint restrictions, muscle weakness, pain, or previous injury—the body automatically finds alternative strategies to complete the desired task.

The kinetic chain principle explains how dysfunction in one area creates compensations throughout the entire movement system. For example, limited ankle dorsiflexion during a squat may cause the body to compensate by increasing forward lean, knee valgus, or early hip flexion to maintain balance and achieve depth.

### Common Compensation Patterns

**Lower Body Compensations:**
- Knee valgus during squatting movements when hip abductor strength is insufficient
- Excessive forward lean when ankle dorsiflexion or hip flexion mobility is limited
- Hip hiking during single-leg movements when hip abductor control is poor
- Toe walking when calf flexibility is restricted

**Upper Body Compensations:**
- Shoulder elevation and protraction when thoracic extension is limited
- Excessive lumbar extension when thoracic spine mobility is restricted
- Scapular winging when serratus anterior strength is inadequate
- Head forward posture when deep neck flexor strength is poor

### Assessment and Identification

Movement screening tools like the Functional Movement Screen (FMS) or Selective Functional Movement Assessment (SFMA) help identify compensatory patterns before they become problematic. Key assessment principles include:

- Observing movement from multiple angles (frontal, sagittal, transverse planes)
- Comparing bilateral movements to identify asymmetries
- Testing both active and passive range of motion to distinguish between mobility and stability issues
- Assessing movement quality under different loads and speeds

- Study type: Prospective cohort study

## Practical Applications

**Movement Assessment Protocol:**
1. Screen fundamental movement patterns (squat, lunge, push, pull, rotation)
2. Identify specific compensations and their likely causes
3. Test isolated joint mobility and muscle strength to confirm limitations
4. Prioritize interventions based on movement hierarchy (mobility before stability before strength)

**Correction Strategies:**
- Address mobility restrictions first through targeted stretching and joint mobilization
- Develop stability and motor control through corrective exercises
- Strengthen weak links in the kinetic chain
- Practice proper movement patterns with progressive loading
- Use cueing and feedback to reinforce correct movement

**Programming Considerations:**
- Begin each session with movement preparation targeting identified limitations
- Include corrective exercises as part of warm-up or cool-down routines
- Progress from isolated corrections to integrated movement patterns
- Regularly reassess to monitor improvement and adjust interventions

## Common Mistakes

⚠️ **Focusing only on the compensation without addressing the root cause** - The visible compensation is often a symptom of underlying dysfunction elsewhere

⚠️ **Attempting to correct multiple patterns simultaneously** - Prioritize the most fundamental limitations first

⚠️ **Ignoring pain or discomfort during movement** - Pain often drives compensatory patterns and must be addressed

⚠️ **Expecting immediate changes in long-standing patterns** - Compensations that have been present for months or years require consistent, patient correction
