### C6. Micronutrients (Detailed)

## Quick Takeaway
Active individuals have higher micronutrient needs than sedentary people, with vitamin D, iron, and B12 being the most commonly deficient nutrients that directly impact performance and recovery.

## Key Points
- Get blood tests for vitamin D and iron status annually if you're an athlete or highly active
- Prioritize whole foods over supplements - a varied diet meets most micronutrient needs
- Female, endurance, and vegetarian/vegan athletes have highest risk for iron and B12 deficiencies
- Vitamin D supplementation (1000-4000 IU daily) is often necessary regardless of diet quality
- Excessive antioxidant supplementation can actually impair training adaptations

## Deep Dive

This note expands on [[../foundation/A4. Micronutrients]], detailing specific vitamins and minerals critical for exercise performance, recovery, and overall health. While balanced diets often meet general population needs, athletes may have increased requirements or specific risks due to higher turnover, losses through sweat, or dietary restrictions (Thomas et al., 2016).

### Priority Micronutrients for Active Individuals

**Vitamin D - The Foundation Nutrient**
- **Primary Functions:** Essential for calcium absorption, bone health [[../documentation/G-VII-1. Musculoskeletal Anatomy Overview]], immune function, and muscle contraction (vitamin D receptors are present in muscle tissue)
- **Food Sources:** Fatty fish (salmon, mackerel), fortified dairy products, egg yolks, fortified cereals
- **Critical Considerations:** Deficiency is widespread globally, affecting up to 50% of athletes. Indoor athletes, those at higher latitudes, individuals with darker skin, and winter training all increase deficiency risk. Low vitamin D status impairs muscle function, bone health, and potentially increases upper respiratory tract infection risk
- **Supplementation Protocol:** 1000-4000 IU daily typically required to achieve optimal blood levels (>75 nmol/L or 30 ng/mL). Status assessment via serum 25(OH)D testing recommended annually

**Iron - The Performance Limiter**
- **Primary Functions:** Core component of hemoglobin (oxygen transport) and myoglobin (muscle oxygen storage), essential for aerobic energy production [[../foundation/A6. Energy Systems (Detailed)]]
- **Food Sources:** Heme iron (highly bioavailable): red meat, poultry, fish. Non-heme iron: legumes, fortified grains, leafy greens, tofu
- **Critical Considerations:** Female, endurance, and vegetarian/vegan athletes face highest deficiency risk due to menstrual losses, increased exercise demands, and lower bioavailability from plant sources. Iron deficiency progresses through three stages: depletion (low ferritin), iron-deficient erythropoiesis, and finally iron-deficiency anemia (low hemoglobin), which severely impairs endurance performance
- **Assessment Protocol:** Annual ferritin screening recommended for at-risk athletes. Supplementation only under medical supervision when deficient

### Essential Supporting Nutrients

**Calcium**
Critical for bone structure and muscle contraction. Dairy products, fortified plant milks, leafy greens provide adequate amounts. Particularly important for athletes at risk of low energy availability [[../hormones_fitness/I3. Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S)]] and female athletes [[../hormones_fitness/I2. Female-Specific Physiological Considerations]].

**Magnesium**
Cofactor in 300+ enzymatic reactions including ATP function and protein synthesis. Found in nuts, seeds, whole grains, leafy greens. Increased sweat losses during exercise may elevate needs, though supplementation benefits in non-deficient individuals remain unclear.

**Zinc**
Essential for immune function, protein synthesis, and wound healing. Meat, shellfish, nuts, and seeds provide adequate amounts. Plant-based athletes may need higher intakes due to reduced bioavailability from phytates.

**B Vitamins**
Function as coenzymes in energy metabolism. Thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, B6, folate widely available in varied diets. **B12 requires special attention** - found almost exclusively in animal products, making supplementation mandatory for vegans [[C11. Vegetarian & Vegan Diets]].

**Antioxidant Vitamins (C, E) and Selenium**
Protect against exercise-induced oxidative stress [[C34. Antioxidants]]. However, high-dose supplementation may blunt beneficial training adaptations. Obtain through colorful fruits, vegetables, nuts, and seeds rather than supplements.

## Practical Applications

**Annual Testing Protocol:**
- Vitamin D (25(OH)D): Target >75 nmol/L (30 ng/mL)
- Iron status (ferritin): Women >30 ng/mL, Men >40 ng/mL
- B12 if vegetarian/vegan: Target >300 pg/mL

**Daily Supplementation Framework:**
- Vitamin D: 1000-4000 IU (adjust based on blood levels)
- Iron: Only if deficient, 18-65mg with vitamin C
- B12 for vegans: 250-500 mcg daily or 2500 mcg weekly

**Food-First Strategy:**
Focus on nutrient-dense whole foods [[C30. Food Quality & Health (Whole vs Processed)]]: colorful vegetables, fruits, lean proteins, whole grains, nuts, seeds provide most micronutrient needs naturally.

## Common Mistakes

⚠️ **Taking iron supplements without testing** - Can cause toxicity and interfere with other mineral absorption

⚠️ **Megadose antioxidant supplementation** - High doses of vitamins C and E may blunt training adaptations

⚠️ **Ignoring B12 as a vegan/vegetarian** - Deficiency develops slowly but causes irreversible neurological damage

⚠️ **Assuming diet alone provides adequate vitamin D** - Even optimal diets rarely provide sufficient amounts

⚠️ **Self-diagnosing deficiencies** - Symptoms overlap with many conditions; blood testing is essential
